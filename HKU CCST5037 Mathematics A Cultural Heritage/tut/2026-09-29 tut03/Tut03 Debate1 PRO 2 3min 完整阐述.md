## 完整稿 (照念)

Okay, real talk. Nobody on our side is saying lock kids in a room and make them chant "seven times eight is fifty-six" all day. That's not what this is about.

What we *are* saying is simple. If you want kids to do harder math later — fractions, algebra, word problems — they need some basic facts sitting ready in their head. Times tables are one of those. Learn the core facts until they just pop out, no heavy thinking.

Here's why that matters. Think about your own head. You can only juggle a few things at once — that's your "mental desk," and it's small. Now picture a kid doing a word problem. The desk is already pretty full. If they also have to stop and count on fingers for every little multiply — boom, the desk overflows. They lose the thread. They give up.

Knowing $7\times 8$ right away? That's like clearing space on the desk so the real thinking can happen. Memorizing facts isn't against thinking. It's what *makes* thinking possible.

And this isn't just one politician's slogan. A lot of school systems that do well in math still want kids to know tables up to ten. Reviews from places like Cambridge Maths treat these facts as building blocks. The evidence on practice is pretty clear too — short sessions, spaced out over days, beat cramming. Work like Woodward's shows the best path isn't "drill only" or "strategies only," it's strategies that grow into fluent recall.

Now, someone will say — what about phones? Phones can multiply. Sure. But in real math, you often need a quick feel for size. You need to spot $6\times 9=54$ while you move an equation around. If every tiny step needs a machine, kids never build number feel.

And here's a picture from my own major, CS. In algorithms we pre-compute answers and store them in a **lookup table**, so the program just pulls the answer instead of working from scratch every time. It's a classic speed trick. Times tables in your head are the same thing — a little lookup table in your brain. You bank the thinking once, then reuse it fast. And to be clear: memorizing facts does NOT mean skipping the why. We want kids to know *why* $7\times 8=56$ too. You memorize the answer *and* understand it — you're not skipping the thinking, you're banking it.

So let me be clear about what we're *not* defending. We're not defending nasty speed contests that rank kids by stopwatch and shame the slow ones. Bad timed drills can hurt — we agree. Throw those out. But that's a teaching problem, not a reason to give up on the goal. The goal still stands: kids should know the core facts, and know them well enough that they don't have to think.

Because here's the thing. Don't call "never memorize" kindness. Sending kids into algebra with no facts ready — that's like sending them in with no tools. And calling that kinder? It's not. It just moves the wall they'll hit later down the road.

So keep the facts. Fix the teaching. Knowing tables by heart isn't the enemy of thinking — empty panic drills are. Thank you.

### 开场

**EN**: Regarding this issue, I have two main viewpoints.

**中**: 关于此问题, 我有两个主要观点。

---

### 第一点 / Point 1

**EN**: First, in real-world mathematical applications, memorizing some basic and common mathematical facts can not only reduce unnecessary calculations and thinking, but also save time and boost efficiency. In fact, when we solve math problems, we already carry too many settled mathematical facts we aren't even conscious of — the sum of two odd numbers is even, even plus even is even, any number times zero is zero. These prior facts are already part of how we understand a math problem. 

My major is CS. In real programming, people often wrap a common piece of code, a useful function, into a library. Calling `sqrt` function to take a square root doesn't mean you don't understand how to compute a square root in code — it just makes programming more efficient. And that leads to my next point.

**中**: 第一, 在实际的数学应用中, 记忆一些基本且常见的数学事实, 不仅能减少不必要的计算与思考, 还能节省时间, 提升效率. 事实上, 我们在解决数学问题时, 本身就已经带有太多自己未曾意识到的既定数学事实了 — 两个奇数之和是偶数, 偶数加偶数还是偶数, 任何数乘零等于零. 这些先验的数学知识本身就是我们理解数学问题的一部分. 我的专业是 CS, 在实际编程中, 人们常常把常用的一段代码, 一个功能包装成库. 调用 `sqrt` 函数来开根号, 并不意味着他们不理解如何用代码给一个整数开根号, 只是这么做能极大提高编程效率. 这也引出我的下一个观点。

---

### 第二点 / Point 2

**EN**: Second, I have to clarify a misunderstanding. Memorizing facts does not mean skipping the why. Memorizing math facts and understanding the principles behind them are not a binary opposition. In fact, memorizing facts can more effectively promote the understanding of principles. 

From a broader, less rigorous angle, the whole mathematical world rests on only a handful of axioms people agree on, and all other theorems and corollaries are derived from those axioms. In other words, most of the math principles you understand can also be seen as math facts derived from axioms. So memorizing facts and understanding principles are not opposites — they reinforce each other.

**中**: 第二, 我要澄清一个误解. 记忆数学事实并不意味着跳过其中的"为什么". 记忆数学事实与理解数学原理并非二元对立. 事实上, 记忆数学事实能更有效地促进对原理的理解. 从一个更广、不太严谨的角度看, 整个数学世界仅有寥寥几条公认公理, 其余的定理、推论都是基于这些公理推导出来的. 也就是说, 你所理解的大部分数学原理, 事实上也可以看作从公理推出的数学事实. 因此, 记忆事实与理解原理不是对立的, 而是相辅相成的。

---

### 收尾 / Close

**EN**: Personally, I believe memorizing math facts like times tables is essentially trading memory for thinking time — a way to simplify real math applications. A school should not only teach mathematical thinking and principles, but also help students handle math problems better in real life. So I support schools teaching students to memorize the math facts they need.

**中**: 我个人认为, 背诵诸如乘法表等的数学事实, 实际上是一种用记忆换取思考时间的行为, 是一种简化实际数学应用的方法. 学校不仅要教会学生数学思维、数学原理, 更要能帮助学生在现实生活中更好地处理数学问题. 所以我支持学校教会学生去记忆自己所需的数学事实。
